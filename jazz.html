R&B and Jazz

Both styles grow from the same roots: the blues, gospel and the 7th chord. Jazz is about moving through chords (and keys) with tension and release; R&B and neo-soul take the same rich chords, slow them down and groove on them. Learn the ideas, the chords and the progressions below, then work through the exercises.

What you should know

Ten ideas that explain most of what you hear in jazz and R&B.

1The 7th chord is home

In pop and rock a chord is usually 3 notes. In jazz and R&B the 4-note 7th chord is the normal chord: C becomes Cmaj7 or C6, Dm becomes Dm7, G becomes G7. Plain triads sound bare in these styles.

2Extensions add colour

Keep stacking 3rds past the 7th and you get the 9th, 11th and 13th: the 2nd, 4th and 6th, an octave up. They don't change what a chord does, only how rich it sounds. R&B lives on the 9th (m9, maj9).

3The 3rd and 7th carry the chord

The 3rd says major or minor, the 7th says what kind of 7th chord it is. Together they're called the guide tones. The 5th adds little and is often left out, and the bass player plays the root, so the pianist or guitarist often leaves that out too.

4ii – V – I is the engine

The V7 chord is full of tension and wants to fall a 5th to I. Put the ii chord in front and you get ii–V–I (Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7 in C). Most jazz standards are chains of ii–V–Is, often moving through several keys.

5Any chord can have its own V7

To make a chord feel like a target, put its V7 in front of it: A7 before Dm7, E7 before Am7. These secondary dominants are why jazz and soul chords so often have notes outside the key.

6Tension is good

Dominant 7ths can be "altered": a ♯9, ♭9 or ♯5 adds grit and makes the pull home stronger. The tritone substitution swaps G7 for D♭7: they share the same 3rd and 7th (B and F), so the bass slides down by semitones: Dm7 – D♭7 – Cmaj7.

7Feel: swing vs. the pocket

Jazz swings: pairs of 8th notes are played long–short, like a triplet with the middle note left out. R&B and neo-soul sit on straight or lightly swung 16ths, often slightly behind the beat. Leaving space is part of the groove: what you don't play matters.

8Voice leading

When the chord changes, move each note the shortest way to the next chord, not the whole hand. Smooth inner lines are what makes these chords sound "expensive". On guitar, small 3–4 string shapes work better than big six-string barre chords.

9A scale for every chord

Over a m7 chord use Dorian, over a dominant 7 Mixolydian, over a maj7 the major scale. The blues scale and the pentatonics work over almost all of R&B. Approach target notes from a semitone below or above for that jazzy sound. Scale shapes has the fingerings.

10It's learned by ear

These styles come from records, not books. Pick a song you love, slow it down, and figure out the chords and a few phrases. Copying the players you like is how everyone learned. Keep them in My songs.

Progressions

Exercises

Do a few of these every practice session. Slow and correct beats fast and sloppy.

1. ii – V – I in every key

Name (or play) the three chords of the ii–V–I in the key shown, then check. Aim to answer in under three seconds. Going round the circle of 4ths is the order jazz tunes move in.

2. Guide tones

Play just the 3rd and 7th of each chord (with the root in the bass, or a friend playing it). Watch how little your hand moves: the 7th of each chord slides down a semitone to become the 3rd of the next. That smooth line is the sound of a ii–V–I.

4. Comping: the Charleston rhythm

"Comping" is accompanying: short chord stabs that leave room for the singer or soloist instead of strumming every beat. Start with the Charleston: hit the chord on beat 1 and on the "and" of 2, then let it rest. Use the voicings from exercise 3. When it feels easy, move the hits around: just the "and" of 4, or beats 2 and 4.

5. More to practise

  1. 7th chords through the key. Play all seven 7th chords in a key, up and down (Chords in a key → set Chords to "7th chords"). Say each name as you play it.
  2. Vamp and improvise. Loop the "Minor 9 vamp" progression above and improvise over it with the Dorian scale or the minor pentatonic. Use only three notes at first and focus on rhythm.
  3. Swing your 8ths. Put on the metronome at a slow tempo, clicks on 2 and 4, and play a scale long–short. Then try the same scale with straight 16ths for an R&B feel.
  4. Play the 12-bar blues. Learn the "Jazz blues" progression above in F and B♭, the two most common keys for jam sessions. Then improvise over it with the blues scale.
  5. Hear the chord types. Can you tell maj7, m7 and 7 apart by ear? Click the chord cards above with your eyes closed, or use the Ear trainer.
  6. Learn songs. Learn one jazz standard and one R&B song you love, by ear if you can. Ballads are a good start: the chords go by slowly.

Reading the Roman numerals: uppercase (V) is a major-type chord, lowercase (ii) minor. ø means half-diminished (m7♭5) and ° diminished. A ♭ or ♯ in front means the chord's root is a semitone lower or higher than the note of the major scale.
