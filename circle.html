Circle of Fifths

All 12 keys in a circle. Each step clockwise goes up a fifth (7 semitones) and adds one sharp; each step anticlockwise adds one flat. Click a key to see its family of chords.

Why it's useful

  • Neighbours sound good together. Keys next to each other share 6 of their 7 notes, so songs often move between them.
  • The family is always a block of 6. A key's three most important chords, I, IV and V, sit side by side: the key itself, the one to its left (IV) and the one to its right (V). Their three minor partners on the inner ring (ii, iii and vi) complete the family. That's why the highlight is always the same shape, whichever key you click.
  • Inner and outer belong together. A major key and the minor key inside it (C major and A minor) use exactly the same notes. They're called relative keys. The difference is which chord feels like "home".
  • Counting sharps and flats. C has none. Go clockwise and each key adds one sharp (G has 1, D has 2…). Go anticlockwise and each key adds one flat (F has 1, B♭ has 2…). At the bottom the two sides meet: F♯ major and G♭ major sound the same, just written differently.
