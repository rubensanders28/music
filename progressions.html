How chord progressions work

  • Roman numerals show where a chord sits in the key: I is the chord on the 1st note of the scale, IV on the 4th, and so on. Upper case is a major chord, lower case is minor, ° is diminished. Because they don't name the actual notes, the same numerals work in every key. Change the key above and the progression moves with it.
  • Home, away, tension. Most progressions leave home (I), wander away (IV, ii), build tension (V) and come back. The colour under each chord shows which job it's doing. Chords with the same job can usually stand in for each other, which is what Swap does.
  • Borrowed chords come from the other key with the same note: ♭VII, ♭VI and iv in a major key are borrowed from the minor key. They make a song sound less predictable, from rock swagger to bittersweet.
  • Loops. Pop songs often repeat one 4-chord loop the whole way through. A progression that ends on tension (V) pulls you straight back to the start, which is why loops work so well.
