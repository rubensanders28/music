Intervals

An interval is the distance between two notes. Every scale, chord and melody is built from them, so once you know intervals, the rest of music theory starts to make sense.

Half steps and whole steps

The smallest step in Western music is a half step (also called a semitone): from one key on the piano to the very next one, black or white, or from one fret to the next on the guitar. Two half steps make a whole step (a tone).

There are 12 different notes before they repeat an octave higher: C, C♯/D♭, D, D♯/E♭, E, F, F♯/G♭, G, G♯/A♭, A, A♯/B♭, B. Notice there's no black key between E and F, or between B and C: those are half steps too.

Every interval is just a number of half steps. A minor 3rd is 3, a major 3rd 4, a perfect 5th 7, an octave 12. Try it below: the orange numbers count the half steps.

Reading the names

The number counts lettersFrom C to E is a 3rd because it spans three letter names: C, D, E. C to G is a 5th: C, D, E, F, G. Count both the first and the last.
The word says exactly how bigTwo 3rds can differ: C to E♭ is a minor 3rd (3 half steps), C to E a major 3rd (4). The letters are the same, the size isn't.
Perfect: 4ths, 5ths and octavesThese sound so pure that they're called perfect, and they don't come in major or minor. One half step bigger is augmented, one smaller is diminished.
Major and minor: 2nds, 3rds, 6ths and 7thsMajor is the bigger one, minor is one half step smaller. In the major scale, every interval from the root is major or perfect.
Same sound, two namesAn augmented 4th and a diminished 5th are both 6 half steps: the tritone. Which name you use depends on the letters: C to F♯ is a 4th (C D E F), C to G♭ is a 5th.
Short namesm3 minor 3rd, M3 major 3rd, P5 perfect 5th, A4 augmented 4th, d5 diminished 5th. Chord symbols use them: the 3 and 5 of a chord are intervals from its root.

Turning an interval upside down

Go up a 3rd from C to E, or go down from C to the E below: that's a 6th. An interval and its upside-down version always add up to 9 (3 + 6, 2 + 7, 4 + 5), and their half steps add up to 12. Major turns into minor and the other way round; perfect stays perfect.

That's why a guitarist can find a note "a 4th up" or "a 5th down" in the same place: they're the same note name.

Where you'll meet them

  • Scales are a pattern of steps. The major scale is whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half; from its root that gives M2, M3, P4, P5, M6, M7 and the octave. See Scale shapes.
  • Chords are stacked intervals. A major chord is a major 3rd with a minor 3rd on top (C E G); a minor chord the other way round (C E♭ G). The Chord library shows every chord's formula.
  • Power chords on the guitar are just a root and a perfect 5th (plus the octave): no 3rd, so neither major nor minor.
  • Harmony sounds sweet or tense because of its intervals: 3rds and 6ths sound warm, 5ths and octaves open and solid, 2nds, 7ths and the tritone tense: they want to move on.

Learning them by ear

  1. Pick two or three intervals to start, for example the major 3rd, the perfect 5th and the octave.
  2. Play them above (up, down and together) and sing along. Connect each one to its song from the table.
  3. Then test yourself in the Ear trainer with only those intervals, and add more as they get easy.
  4. On the guitar, learn the shapes across two strings: they're the same on every pair of strings, except that anything crossing from the G to the B string moves up one fret.
